# Peanutlife house style

Peanutlife is a daily good-news site. Readers arrive tired of the news. Our job is to show them, truthfully, what got better today, and to leave them calmer and a little more capable than when they arrived.

## Voice

- Plain, warm, specific. Write like a thoughtful friend who reads widely, not a brand.
- Short sentences. One idea per sentence. Concrete nouns and numbers over adjectives.
- Never use: heartwarming, uplifting, inspiring, amazing, incredible, faith in humanity, feel-good, wholesome, chills, tears. Show the thing; don't label the feeling.
- No exclamation marks. No rhetorical questions. No "imagine a world where".
- British or American spelling follows the edition: US edition uses American spelling, Global and East Asia editions use British spelling.
- Name the people and places. "A Kyoto nurse" beats "a woman".
- Keep the caveats. If a treatment is in trials, say "in trials". If a law passed one chamber, say so. Good news that overstates is bad news later.

## What counts as good news

Something got better, was repaired, protected, discovered, or done well, and it is true. Progress with a direction, not just a pleasant event.

Yes: a species recovering, a treatment reaching patients, a bill that helps people passing with votes from both sides, a community solving its own problem, an act of generosity, a record of human skill or endurance, a wrong put right.

No: product launches and reviews, celebrity news, business results, listicles and tips, opinion pieces, motivational advice, anything whose main event is a death, injury, crime, disaster or war even with a hopeful ending, campaign and election-horse-race coverage, outrage framed as positive, advertising dressed as news, stories whose only "good" is that something bad didn't happen.

Unsure: a story about a problem with a promising fix is good news if the fix is the point. A story about a tragedy with a kind response is not, for us, unless the response is years on and the point of the piece.

## Sections

- kids: safe to read aloud to a six-year-old. No death, injury, crime, abuse, disasters, war, politics, romance, medical detail. Animals, nature, kindness, invention, sport and space are the usual fits.
- politics: laws passed, deals signed, services delivered, corruption fixed, rights extended, peace steps. Never candidates, polls, campaigns, speeches, or one side beating another.
- nature: wildlife, oceans, forests, rivers, climate repair, clean energy that is built or working (not announced).
- sports: comebacks, firsts, sportsmanship, participation. The result is the least of it.
- science: discoveries and treatments with a plausible path to real people. Keep the trial stage in the copy.
- kindness: ordinary people doing something generous for others, usually unasked.
- culture: art, music, books, craft, language, heritage, food traditions: made, saved or shared.
- progress: everything else that measurably improved: poverty, literacy, safety, infrastructure, technology that is already helping.

## The pieces we write per story

- headline: 6 to 12 words, plain, present tense where natural. States what happened. No clickbait, no colons, no puns.
- one_line: one sentence, under 25 words, that could stand alone in a list of ten.
- in_short: 2 or 3 bullets, each one fact, each under 20 words. Numbers and names if the source has them.
- why_card: one sentence, under 20 words, beginning with the substance, not with "This". What it changes for whom.
- why_long: 2 or 3 sentences on the significance. Specific to this story. No generic hope language.
- before / now: one short sentence each. The situation before, the situation after. If the story is a single event with no before, leave both empty.
- action: optional. One concrete thing a reader could do in under ten minutes, tied to the story, only if a real one exists (an open protocol to share, a volunteer scheme, a petition that is live, a way to donate to the named organisation). Otherwise empty. Never invent an organisation.
- kid_version: only when kid_safe. Three sentences a six-year-old could follow, plus one "talk about it" question.

## The daily letter

120 to 180 words from the editor. Finds a through-line in the day's ten (three stories about things coming back; two about young people fixing what adults broke). Points to one section worth a look. Honest about the mood of the week if the news has been hard. Never summarises all ten. Ends on something plain, not a flourish. Signed "The editor".

## Section standfirsts

One sentence each, under 22 words, saying what is in that section today and why it is worth a minute. Rewritten daily so it reflects what is actually there.
